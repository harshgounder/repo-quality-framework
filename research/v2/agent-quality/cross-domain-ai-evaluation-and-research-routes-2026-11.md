# Cross-domain AI evaluation and research routes — 2026-11-10

**Status:** source-linked exploratory evidence across AI research discovery, lab artifacts, scientific agents, computer-use agents and reliability methods. It is not a complete map of AI labs or all agent work. Most numeric findings below come from papers or owner documentation and were not independently replicated here.

## Why widen beyond coding agents

Repository quality is only one possible target of an AI-enabled system. AI labs and independent groups also evaluate scientific workflows, computer-use agents, security boundaries, research reproduction, and reliable behavior across repeated or perturbed runs. These studies offer candidate evaluation mechanisms and failure models; each must still be checked against the actual system and domain before reuse.

The follow-up [AI-generated repository maintainability and lab-artifact review](ai-generated-repository-maintainability-and-lab-artifacts-2026-11.md) adds recent software-engineering studies, the NITR benchmark and its current repository release, a Korean/East Asian AI-for-AI benchmark, an agent-first public codebase, and field evidence about post-merge quality and issue persistence. These sources strengthen the software-repository and source-coverage dimensions; they do not close the broader survey.

## Cross-domain primary evidence sampled

### Long-horizon scientific software and data workflows

[OmicsBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21074) is an independent University of Michigan/AfterQuery preprint (2026-10-15). It comprises 97 computational-biology study tasks, represented as 662 dependent subtasks in directed acyclic graphs and evaluated over multi-turn sessions. The authors evaluated 12 models on three harnesses; their reported per-task averages reach 3.6 hours, 350 tool calls and 110 million tokens for some configurations. The benchmark uses 58 biologically informed graders rather than string matching for named entities, with deterministic answer keys; the paper reports declining performance on dependent later subtasks and partial recovery with feedback. Three tasks and graders are released while 94 tasks are held out. This is a strong example of workflow history, domain-aware oracle design, repeated turns and resource budget being part of the evaluation unit. Its narrow bioinformatics scope, private majority split, and deterministic score's lack of qualitative diagnosis constrain interpretation.

[GeneBench-Pro](https://openai.com/index/introducing-genebench-pro) is an OpenAI research release dated 2026-06-30. The page describes 129 problems across 10 domains and 21 subdomains, judgment-heavy analysis of messy computational-biology data, detailed case studies and expert review outcomes, with 10 representative questions released publicly and a 50-question independent-benchmark subset planned. It reports a best stated pass rate of 31.5% for the tested GPT-5.6 Sol Pro configuration and says typical human experts estimated 20–40 hours per problem. The authors explicitly discuss ambiguity in analysis choices and the need for solver contracts. These are vendor-reported results from the tested version and benchmark; the proposed third-party comparison was not confirmed in this pass.

[LifeVal](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/lifeval.pdf), an OpenAI paper dated 2026-11-03, evaluates personalized consumer tasks using task-specific context and histories. Its design treats user preference, memory and success criteria as part of the task; its paper reports human-versus-LLM rubric grading on a 30-task subset (17 trainers) and notes remaining variation in browsing runs. It is a useful reminder that correctness may depend on user context and that rubric quality and grading agreement need their own validation. The paper's use of ChatGPT histories is a sensitive-data and consent dimension that requires separate privacy and governance review; this note does not evaluate that protocol.

[ReproBAIT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19810) is an October 2026 preprint by the UK AI Security Institute and SecureBio. It gives coding agents scientific publications or abstracts, training data, a fixed time/compute budget and a no-internet sandbox, then evaluates the reproduced biological AI tool on held-out data with tool-specific metrics. The three target tools range from ProteinMPNN through RFdiffusion3 to a closed-source molecular-design tool. The authors report meaningful reproduction for some selected tools, much weaker performance for the most complex tool, and moderate evidence consistent with memorization for some systems. This reframes “code task success” as reproducibility of a trained artifact under a domain outcome metric, while making data leakage, dual-use exposure, GPU budget and private-tool ownership explicit. The study is a small selected set; the blog's “near original performance on two of three” summary is not a broad reproduction rate.

### Desktop agents: safe execution can diverge from task completion

[OSGuard](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15034), a UC Santa Cruz study revised 2026-10-09, evaluates both 324 human-annotated action examples and 45 risk-augmented execution tasks derived from 40 OSWorld tasks. It reports 79.9% accuracy and 0.80 macro-F1 for the strongest tested action-level guardrail. In full-task evaluation, an unguarded executor completed 62.2% safely while 37.8% were unsafe completions; the strongest added guardrail reduced unsafe completion to 33.3% without changing safe success. This explicitly separates local action judgments from outcomes over a complete interactive task. It is not evidence that the benchmark exhausts possible desktop safety risks or that its tested guardrail is production-ready.

[ATBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02022), published at COLM 2026 and led by Shanghai AI Lab with academic collaborators, contains 1,000 agent trajectories (503 labeled safe, 497 unsafe), averaging 9.01 turns and 3.95k tokens, with 1,954 invoked tools drawn from a pool of 2,084. The paper reports rule/LLM filtering plus a human full audit and evaluates both coarse safety classification and fine-grained risk-source, failure-mode and harm diagnosis; GPT-5.4 is reported at 76.7% F1 on binary classification and 33.6% on fine-grained risk-source diagnosis. The repository and dataset are linked from the paper. Synthetic/constructed trajectory realism, label quality, tool-pool selection and cross-benchmark comparability remain appraisal questions.

[Windows Agent Arena](https://github.com/microsoft/WindowsAgentArena) is a Microsoft open environment for multimodal desktop agents. Its repository describes a Windows 11 virtual-machine snapshot, Docker/QEMU setup and optional Azure parallelism; the documented golden image is about 30 GB, and the README gives separate compute and model-run cost examples. This makes environment preparation, snapshot/versioning, task setup, infrastructure cost and local/scale equivalence visible parts of a system benchmark. The repository points to a 2024 technical report; README statements and current run instructions were inspected, but the environment and benchmark were not executed.

### Reliability is more than average task success

[Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16666), published in ICML 2026, proposes 12 metrics across consistency, robustness, predictability and safety, and evaluates 15 models across two benchmarks. It operationalizes repeated-run consistency, sensitivity to environment/input perturbations, predictability of failure and bounded failure severity. The authors report that reliability gains lag accuracy gains in their selected benchmark/model sample. This is a useful measurement proposal, not yet a universally validated score: each dimension has different units, consequence assumptions, perturbations and oracle requirements, and the paper's two benchmark contexts do not establish coverage of arbitrary deployed systems.

## AI research discovery and artifact routes sampled

### Discovery surfaces

- [The Latest in AI](https://thelatestinai.com/) groups arXiv research by topic. The retrieved landing/feed was marked as an early preview and showed papers through 2026-11-06 on the 2026-11-10 retrieval date. It is a discovery index, not a completeness guarantee or primary evidence source.
- [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/) was used for recency-prioritized paper discovery and targeted paper-page questions. Its returned rankings were bounded and truncated; the linked arXiv paper/version remains the evidence target.
- [Awesome Frontier AI Papers](https://github.com/LikeACloud7/awesome-frontier-ai-papers) is a community tracker that, in the README snapshot fetched here, indexed 34,646 publications from 19 configured US/China labs, with its latest snapshot dated 2026-11-05. The public [coverage policy](https://github.com/LikeACloud7/awesome-frontier-ai-papers/blob/main/docs/COVERAGE.md) describes daily collection from official catalogues/feeds, OpenAlex, Hugging Face, and rotating repositories/affiliation searches, plus a 19-day historical-reconciliation cycle. It explicitly states it cannot prove complete recall and excludes company publications that are not AI-scoped or not attributed to the configured labs.

The tracker's public [collection-health snapshot](https://github.com/LikeACloud7/awesome-frontier-ai-papers/blob/main/public/data/collection_health.json), checked in this pass, was marked `partial`: 111 sources were listed (64 `ok`, 43 `partial`, 4 `failed`); 35 sources had recorded errors (31 partial plus 4 failed); 1,359 records were pending metadata. The newest check timestamp was 2026-11-05 11:23:30 UTC. The repository's workflow is configured for daily runs but the retrieved data snapshot was five days old. This is a concrete freshness/health lesson: a scheduled workflow, indexed record count, and current collection run are different claims. The publication data file itself was too large for the connected contents route and returned empty content, so item-level freshness was not independently measured.

These routes complement, but do not replace, primary channels such as [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/), [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/), [Hugging Face Papers](https://huggingface.co/papers), [Papers with Code](https://paperswithcode.com/), and official lab research/release feeds. Their indexing rules, date semantics, update cadence, language coverage, author-affiliation resolution, and artifact-link policies need separate, versioned audits.

### Lab pages and public code artifacts

- OpenAI's [shared playbook for trustworthy third-party evaluations](https://openai.com/index/trustworthy-third-party-evaluations-foundations/) argues that reports identify the claim being tested and evidence for result validity; it calls out the model, harness, tools, budget, scoring, task set, contamination, reward hacking, and known limits. This is first-party guidance from an evaluated-model provider, not an independent consensus standard.
- The [OpenAI Evals repository](https://github.com/openai/evals) describes an eval registry and custom/private evals, Git-LFS data, model/system evaluation templates and cost considerations. Its README also specifies that contributed evaluation logic/data are under the repository license and that contributors need rights to upload data; data use terms must be read before contribution. This was a README-level inspection, not a current code or product compatibility audit.
- The [Anthropic `scone-bench` repository](https://github.com/anthropics/scone-bench) says it is not maintained and does not accept contributions. It describes 417 smart-contract exploit tasks, with a 12-task post-cutoff subset, a local forked EVM setup and a grader that restarts the chain before scoring. It documents roughly 2 GB RAM per task container and a five-hour full-run wall-clock budget. This is a valuable objective-oracle and anti-cheating design example, while its explicit maintenance status, infrastructure needs and selected DeFi incident population are material limitations.
- Microsoft's [Windows Agent Arena repository](https://github.com/microsoft/WindowsAgentArena) is a second direct example of environment-backed evaluation. The same README describes the VM image, setup, local and Azure execution paths, agent configurations and task-development contribution route. Code, tests, task validators and current cloud workflows were not audited.

## Research-design implications — project inference

The sampled work suggests the research model needs distinct axes for:

1. **System and consequence:** code edits, deployed software, desktop control, scientific analysis, model training/reproduction, external services, data and hardware.
2. **Evaluation unit:** single action, task, trajectory, multi-turn workflow, dependency graph, complete trained artifact, downstream user outcome, or longitudinal deployment.
3. **Context and state:** task history, user preferences, permissions, repository precedents, environment snapshot, data lineage, hidden state and external side effects.
4. **Oracle and adjudication:** deterministic answer keys, state-diff checks, domain-informed graders, human rubric, expert review, outcome metrics, unacceptable side effects, and `unable/ambiguous` classifications.
5. **Repeatability and stress:** repeated runs, alternate tool configurations, delayed triggers, retries, environment perturbations, resource ceilings, unsafe-neighbor/context exposure and cross-task dependencies.
6. **Research artifact:** model/version, harness, toolset, system prompt, task/data version, environment image, scorer, checker, labels, source attribution, license/privacy, run budget and code/data availability.
7. **Collection quality:** configured source universe, supported languages/regions, successful/partial/failed source pulls, pagination/queue state, attribution evidence, last successful update and item-level publication date.

This is a research plan, not a final scoring model. A benchmark that passes its own tests may still have an invalid task, weak oracle, inappropriate workload, stale data, incomplete source universe or unacceptable side effects. Conversely, a useful domain-specific benchmark is not a universal repository gate.
